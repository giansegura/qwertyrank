If you have ever taken a typing test and wondered whether your number is good, you are not alone. The honest answer is that there is no single number, but there are typical ranges, and knowing them helps you set a realistic goal.

This guide walks through what average typing speed usually looks like, what pushes it up or down, and how to measure yours in a way you can trust.

## What average typing speed looks like

Typing speed is usually measured in words per minute (WPM), where a "word" is counted as five characters. If you want the details, our guide on [WPM vs CPM](/en/guides/wpm-vs-cpm) explains how the numbers are calculated.

The ranges below are rough and approximate. They describe what you tend to see, not hard rules:

- **Beginners and occasional typers:** often somewhere around 20–30 WPM, especially if they look at the keyboard or type with a few fingers.
- **Most adults:** commonly land somewhere around 35–45 WPM on a physical keyboard. This is the range people usually mean when they talk about "average."
- **Students:** vary widely. Many who write a lot for school sit in the 35–55 WPM range, while others who mostly use phones may be slower on a full keyboard.
- **Office work:** people who type all day often reach roughly 50–70 WPM. Many jobs that involve a lot of typing are comfortable in this range.
- **Professional typists:** transcriptionists and others who type for a living commonly work at around 70–90 WPM or more, with high accuracy.
- **Top competitive typists:** the fastest people on short tests can go well beyond 120 WPM, and a small group reaches far higher in bursts.

If your score falls in the middle of these ranges, you are typing at a perfectly normal speed. If it is lower, that is simply a starting point.

## What affects your typing speed

Two people with the same amount of experience can still post very different scores. Here are the main factors.

### Practice and technique

The biggest single factor is how much you type and how you do it. Touch typing, meaning typing without looking at the keys and using all ten fingers, tends to raise your ceiling a lot. Hunt-and-peck typing can be surprisingly quick for some people, but it usually plateaus earlier. If you want to learn the standard hand position, see our guide on [finger placement](/en/guides/finger-placement).

### The keyboard you use

A physical keyboard and a phone screen are very different tools. Most people type noticeably slower on a touch keyboard, mainly because they use one or two thumbs instead of ten fingers. That is why comparing a phone score with a desktop score is not really fair. Our guide on [physical vs touch keyboards](/en/guides/physical-vs-touch-keyboard) covers this in more detail.

Even among physical keyboards, the layout and feel matter. A keyboard you are used to will almost always give you a better result than one you have just sat down at.

### Language and accents

Typing in your native language is usually faster, because familiar words flow more easily from memory to fingers. Languages with accents or special letters add extra keystrokes or key combinations, which can lower speed a little until they become automatic.

### Accuracy

Speed without accuracy does not mean much. Every mistake costs time to notice and correct, and in most typing tests errors also reduce your final score. Many people find that slowing down slightly to make fewer mistakes actually raises their WPM.

### Test length and text

Short tests tend to produce higher numbers than long ones, because it is easier to stay focused for 30 seconds than for several minutes. The kind of text matters too: common lowercase words with no punctuation are easier than sentences full of capitals, numbers and symbols. Keep this in mind when you compare scores from different sites.

## How to measure your speed honestly

If you want a number you can trust and compare over time, a few habits help:

1. **Use the same test each time.** Different sites count words and errors differently, so stick with one.
2. **Take more than one attempt.** Your first try is often affected by nerves or by getting used to the screen. Look at your typical result, not just a single lucky run.
3. **Pay attention to accuracy.** A fast score with poor accuracy is not a fair picture of how you type.
4. **Type the way you normally would.** Do not rely on tricks like autocorrect or word suggestions on a phone. Typing letter by letter gives a real measure of your skill.

## What is a good goal?

A good goal depends on where you are now and what you need typing for. Some general ideas:

- **If you are under 30 WPM,** aim to reach the 35–45 WPM range with good accuracy. This is where everyday typing starts to feel comfortable.
- **If you are around 40 WPM,** reaching 50–60 WPM is a solid target and makes a real difference for emails, school work and chat.
- **If you are already above 60 WPM,** gains come more slowly. Focus on consistency, accuracy and the specific keys or letter pairs that trip you up.

Whatever your level, aim for steady progress rather than a specific number by a specific date. Everyone improves at a different pace. Our guide on [how to type faster](/en/guides/how-to-type-faster) has a practical plan you can follow.
