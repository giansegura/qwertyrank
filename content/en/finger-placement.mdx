Where you put your fingers on the keyboard has a big effect on your typing speed. Correct finger placement is the foundation of touch typing: it lets you type without looking down, keeps your hands relaxed and gives every finger a clear job.

This guide explains the standard hand position on a QWERTY keyboard, which finger presses which key, and how to make the habit stick.

## The home row

Everything starts from the **home row**, the middle row of letters. It is where your fingers rest when you are not pressing anything, and where they return after every keystroke.

Place your fingers like this:

- **Left hand:** pinky on A, ring finger on S, middle finger on D, index finger on F.
- **Right hand:** index finger on J, middle finger on K, ring finger on L, pinky on the semicolon key.
- **Thumbs:** resting lightly on the space bar.

Your fingers should be gently curved, as if you were holding a small ball, with the fingertips just touching the keys.

## The bumps on F and J

Look closely at the F and J keys on almost any keyboard and you will find a small raised bump or line. These are there for a reason: they let your index fingers find the home row by touch.

Whenever you lose your place, you do not need to look down. Feel for the bumps with your index fingers, let the other fingers settle next to them, and carry on.

## Which finger presses which key

On a standard keyboard, the keys are split into diagonal columns, and each finger covers one or two of them. From the home row, each finger reaches up and down to the keys in its column.

### Left hand

- **Pinky:** Q, A, Z, plus the 1 key and the keys on the far left, such as Tab, Caps Lock and the left Shift.
- **Ring finger:** W, S, X and the 2 key.
- **Middle finger:** E, D, C and the 3 key.
- **Index finger:** R, F, V and also T, G, B, plus the 4 and 5 keys. The index finger covers two columns because it is the strongest and most agile finger.

### Right hand

- **Index finger:** U, J, M and also Y, H, N, plus the 6 and 7 keys.
- **Middle finger:** I, K, the comma key and the 8 key.
- **Ring finger:** O, L, the period key and the 9 key.
- **Pinky:** P, the semicolon, the slash, the 0 key and the keys on the far right, such as the apostrophe, the brackets, Enter, Backspace and the right Shift.

### Thumbs

Your thumbs handle the space bar. Most people use one thumb consistently, usually the one on their dominant side, but either works as long as it feels natural and you do not have to move your hand.

## Using the Shift keys

When you need a capital letter or a symbol, use the Shift key on the **opposite** side from the letter you are typing. For a capital A, which is typed with the left pinky, hold the right Shift with your right pinky. For a capital J, hold the left Shift with your left pinky.

This keeps both hands balanced and avoids awkward stretches with one hand.

## Wrists and posture

Good finger placement works best with a relaxed, comfortable posture:

- **Keep your wrists straight** and slightly raised above the keyboard. Avoid bending them up, down or to the side.
- **Do not rest the heels of your hands on the desk while typing.** A wrist rest is fine during pauses, but your hands should float while you type.
- **Sit at a height** where your elbows form roughly a right angle and your forearms are close to parallel with the floor.
- **Keep your shoulders relaxed** and your back supported.
- **Place the keyboard** directly in front of you, so the B key lines up roughly with the middle of your body.

If you feel tension or pain, stop and adjust your setup.

## How long does it take to learn?

It varies from person to person. Most people can learn where each finger belongs in a few days of practice, but making it automatic takes longer, often several weeks of regular practice.

At first you will probably type more slowly than before. That is completely normal: your old way of typing feels fast because it is familiar, while the new one has to be built from scratch. Many people find that their speed catches up after a while and eventually passes their old level.

A few tips to get through the learning phase:

1. **Practice a little every day** rather than for long sessions once in a while.
2. **Do not look at the keyboard,** even when you are unsure. Use the bumps on F and J instead.
3. **Go slowly and accurately.** Speed will come from correct, repeated movements.
4. **Always return to the home row** after each key.

## A note on keyboard layouts

This guide describes the standard QWERTY layout used for English. Keyboards sold in different countries can vary slightly, for example in where the symbol keys, the Enter key or the quote marks sit. The home row and the letter positions stay the same on English QWERTY keyboards, so the finger assignments above still apply.

If you also type in other languages, you may need extra keys or combinations for accented letters. Those keys usually fall under the pinky or are reached with dead keys, but the core position on the home row does not change.

The English test on QwertyRank uses common English words in lowercase, without punctuation, so you can focus on letters and the space bar while you build the habit.
