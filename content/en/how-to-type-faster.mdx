Most people who want to type faster simply try to push harder. That rarely works for long. Real improvement comes from better habits, a bit of structure and regular short practice.

This guide gives you a practical plan you can start today, plus a simple way to track your progress with a typing test.

## Start with accuracy, not speed

It sounds backwards, but the fastest way to type faster is to slow down first. Every mistake costs more than the wrong keystroke: you have to notice it, delete and retype.

There is a longer-term reason too: practicing with lots of errors trains your fingers to make them, while clean practice builds reliable movements that get quicker over time.

A good rule of thumb is to aim for 95% accuracy or better while practicing. If you keep dropping below that, ease off the pace until the mistakes settle down.

## Learn to touch type

Touch typing means typing without looking at the keyboard, using all ten fingers, each responsible for its own group of keys. If you currently type with two or three fingers, this is the change with the biggest potential.

The basics:

- Rest your fingers on the home row: left hand on A, S, D and F, right hand on J, K, L and the semicolon.
- Find the small bumps on F and J with your index fingers. They let you return to position without looking.
- Each finger moves up and down from its home key to reach the keys above and below it.
- Your thumbs handle the space bar.

Our guide on [finger placement](/en/guides/finger-placement) covers exactly which finger presses which key.

Expect to get slower for a while when you switch. That is normal: your old habits are fast because they are familiar, and the new ones need time to catch up.

## Stop looking at the keyboard

Looking down breaks your rhythm and keeps your fingers from learning where the keys are.

A few ways to break the habit:

- Keep your eyes on the screen, even when you are unsure. Make the mistake and learn from it.
- If you really cannot resist, cover your hands loosely with a cloth or a piece of paper while practicing.
- Use the bumps on F and J to reset your position instead of glancing down.

## Set up a comfortable posture

You will type faster and longer if you are comfortable. Small adjustments help:

- Sit with your back supported and your feet flat on the floor.
- Keep your elbows roughly at a right angle and your shoulders relaxed.
- Let your wrists float a little above the keyboard instead of pressing them into the desk.
- Place the screen at about eye level so you are not hunching forward.

Tension is the enemy of speed, so shake out your hands if they feel tight.

## Practice a little, every day

Short, regular sessions beat long, occasional ones. Ten to fifteen minutes a day is plenty for most people, and it is much easier to keep up than an hour once a week.

A simple daily routine could look like this:

1. **Warm up** with a few short runs at a relaxed pace.
2. **Focused practice** on the keys or letter combinations that slow you down.
3. **A couple of timed tests** to see how you are doing.
4. **Stop** while you still feel fresh.

## Work on your problem keys and bigrams

Everyone has a few keys that slow them down. It might be a letter far from the home row, a key you always hit with the wrong finger, or a common letter pair (a bigram) like "th", "er" or "ou" that never feels smooth.

Pay attention to where you hesitate or make mistakes during a test. Then practice those keys and pairs on their own, slowly and correctly, until they feel natural. Once they stop being weak spots, your overall speed rises with them.

## Find a steady rhythm

Fast typists rarely type in bursts followed by pauses. They keep an even, steady rhythm, like walking at a comfortable pace instead of sprinting and stopping.

Try to keep the same tempo for easy and hard words. If a word is tricky, slow down slightly for it rather than rushing and making a mistake. Over time, the tempo you can hold steadily will rise.

It also helps to read a word or two ahead. While your fingers finish one word, your eyes should already be on the next.

## Rest and recover

Your hands need rest, and skills often settle in between sessions, which is one reason daily practice works better than cramming.

If you feel pain or discomfort in your hands, wrists or arms, stop and rest. Pushing through pain does not make you faster and can cause problems that keep you away from the keyboard for much longer.

## Track your progress with QwertyRank

Measuring your progress keeps you motivated and shows whether your practice is working. QwertyRank gives you two tools for this.

The [15-second practice mode](/en/practice) is for low-pressure drilling. It has no ranking, so you can warm up, try a new technique or focus on accuracy without worrying about your score.

The [ranked typing test](/en) lasts 30 seconds after a 3-second countdown. Your WPM counts only correct characters, and your accuracy is the share of keystrokes you got right. With at least 90% accuracy, your result can enter the leaderboard for your language and keyboard type, and only your best game counts. That makes it a good way to check in every few days without stressing over a single bad run.

Look at the trend over weeks rather than any single day; some days will simply be slower. If WPM and CPM are confusing, our guide on [WPM vs CPM](/en/guides/wpm-vs-cpm) explains how they are calculated.

## Be patient

Typing speed improves gradually. Some people see quick gains in the first weeks, others progress more slowly, and both are normal. How fast you improve depends on where you start and how much you practice.
