Most of us type on two very different kinds of keyboard every day: a physical one on a laptop or desktop, and a touch keyboard on a phone or tablet. They both produce text, but the way you use them, and the speed you can reach, are not the same at all.

This guide explains what changes between the two, what typical speeds look like on each, and why QwertyRank keeps a separate leaderboard for each kind of keyboard.

## How typing differs on each keyboard

### Fingers vs thumbs

On a physical keyboard, a trained typist uses all ten fingers. Each finger has its own keys, and both hands work at the same time, so one hand can be getting ready for the next letter while the other is still pressing. Our guide on [finger placement](/en/guides/finger-placement) explains how this works.

On a phone, most people type with one or two thumbs. Thumbs are less precise and have to travel across the whole keyboard, so there is far less overlap between keystrokes. On a tablet, some people type with several fingers, but the flat screen still makes it harder than on real keys.

### Feel and feedback

Physical keys move when you press them. You can feel where one key ends and the next begins, the home row has bumps on F and J, and you know a key has registered without looking. That is what makes typing without looking at the keyboard possible.

A touch screen is flat glass. Some phones add a small vibration (haptic feedback) or a click sound to each tap, which helps, but you still cannot feel the edges of the keys. Most people need to glance at the keyboard much more often on a touch screen.

### Autocorrect and predictive text

Touch keyboards come with tools designed to make up for their limits:

- **Autocorrect** fixes small mistakes automatically, often replacing a whole word.
- **Predictive text** suggests the next word, which you can insert with a single tap.
- **Swipe typing** lets you draw a line across the letters and the keyboard guesses the word.

These are great for everyday messages, but when the keyboard writes a word for you, you are no longer typing every letter yourself.

Physical keyboards generally do not do any of this. What you press is what you get.

## Typical speed differences

Speeds vary a lot from person to person, so these are only approximate ranges:

- On a **physical keyboard**, most adults type somewhere around 35–45 WPM, and practiced touch typists often go well above that.
- On a **phone**, many people type somewhere around 25–40 WPM with their thumbs when they type letter by letter, and frequent phone users can be quicker.
- On a **tablet**, results tend to fall somewhere in between, depending on how you hold it and how many fingers you use.

For most people, their physical keyboard speed is noticeably higher than their phone speed. But not for everyone: some people who grew up texting and rarely use a computer are actually faster on a phone. For a broader look at typical ranges, see our guide on [average typing speed](/en/guides/average-typing-speed).

## Why QwertyRank has separate leaderboards

Because the two kinds of keyboard are so different, putting them on the same leaderboard would not be fair. A good phone typist would never appear near the top of a list dominated by people typing with ten fingers on a mechanical keyboard, and a phone score of 60 WPM can be just as impressive as a much higher score on a physical keyboard.

That is why QwertyRank has **one leaderboard per language and keyboard type**. You can see the fastest typists on a [physical keyboard](/en/leaderboard/physical) and on a [touch keyboard](/en/leaderboard/touch) separately, and you can appear on both if you play on both kinds of device. On each one, only your best game counts, and you need at least 90% accuracy to enter.

### The server decides which keyboard you used

You do not choose which leaderboard your game goes to. Instead, the server looks at how the keys were pressed during your game and decides whether you typed on a physical or a touch keyboard.

This keeps the leaderboards honest. It also means that what matters is how you actually typed, not which device you used: if you connect a physical keyboard to a tablet, for example, your game is judged by your keystrokes.

Scores fast enough to enter a top 10 are verified with a second game, whose text is drawn as an image. That extra step helps keep the top of each leaderboard trustworthy.

## Why autocorrect and suggestions do not count in ranked

The ranked test measures how fast you type, letter by letter. If a word appears because you tapped a suggestion, swiped across the keyboard or let autocorrect replace it, you did not type those letters yourself, and the result would not be comparable with everyone else's.

So in the ranked test, you need to type each letter on its own. A game that relies on inserting several letters at once will not count.

A few tips for playing on a phone or tablet:

1. **Turn off swipe typing** while you play, so a stray drag does not insert a whole word.
2. **Ignore the suggestion bar** and keep tapping letter by letter.
3. **Consider turning off autocorrect** in your keyboard settings if it keeps changing words as you type.
4. **Hold your device the same way each time** so your results are consistent.

The test uses common words of the language, lowercase and without punctuation, so you will not need to switch to the symbols keyboard or press Shift.

## Practice on both

If you want to improve on either keyboard, the same principles apply: accuracy first, a steady rhythm and short, regular practice. Our guide on [how to type faster](/en/guides/how-to-type-faster) covers these in detail, and most of the advice works just as well with thumbs as with ten fingers.

## Try it on your device

Take the [30-second typing test](/en) on your computer, then try it on your phone. Comparing the two is a good way to see how much the keyboard itself shapes your speed. If you want to warm up first, the [15-second practice](/en/practice) has no ranking, so you can get used to the test without any pressure.
