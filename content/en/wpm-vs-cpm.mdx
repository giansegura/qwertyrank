When you take a typing test or a WPM test, the result usually comes as a number followed by "WPM", and sometimes "CPM" as well. Both measure the same thing, how fast you type, but on different scales. Once you know how they relate, it is easy to compare scores and understand what your result really means.

## What WPM means

WPM stands for **words per minute**. It is the most common way to express typing speed, and it is what most people mean when they say "I type 60 words per minute."

There is a catch, though. Real words come in very different lengths: "a" is one letter, "information" is eleven. If a test counted actual words, typing a text full of short words would look much faster than typing one full of long words, even at the same pace.

To make results comparable, typing tests use a standard convention: **one word equals five characters**. Spaces count as characters too. So when a test says you typed 60 WPM, it means you typed the equivalent of 300 characters in one minute, regardless of how many actual words that was.

## What CPM means

CPM stands for **characters per minute**. It simply counts every character you type in a minute, without grouping them into words. Some people prefer it because it is more direct, and it is common in certain countries and in some professional typing tests.

## How to convert between WPM and CPM

Because a word is defined as five characters, converting is simple:

- **CPM = WPM × 5**
- **WPM = CPM divided by 5**

A few examples:

- 40 WPM is 200 CPM.
- 60 WPM is 300 CPM.
- 80 WPM is 400 CPM.
- 350 CPM is 70 WPM.

If you see two scores on different scales, convert one of them before comparing. A "250" in CPM is not faster than a "60" in WPM; it is actually slower, because 60 WPM equals 300 CPM.

## Raw WPM vs net WPM

Many typing tests show more than one speed. The two you will see most often are raw WPM and net WPM (sometimes just called WPM).

**Raw WPM** counts everything you typed, mistakes included. It tells you how fast your fingers were moving.

**Net WPM** only counts what you typed correctly, or subtracts a penalty for errors, depending on the site. It tells you how much useful text you actually produced.

The gap between the two is a good sign of how much accuracy is costing you. If your raw WPM is much higher than your net WPM, you are losing a lot of speed to mistakes, and slowing down slightly will probably raise your net score. ## What accuracy measures

Accuracy is usually shown as a percentage: the share of your keystrokes that were correct. If you pressed 200 keys and 190 of them were right, your accuracy is 95%.

An important detail: on some tests, including QwertyRank, a mistake still counts against your accuracy even if you go back and fix it. The wrong keystroke happened, so it is part of the total. Correcting errors keeps your WPM from dropping, but it does not erase them from your accuracy.

## Why different sites give different results

If you have taken typing tests on several websites, you may have noticed that your scores do not match. That does not necessarily mean one of them is wrong. They often just measure in slightly different ways:

- **How errors are handled.** Some sites only count correct characters, others subtract a fixed penalty per error, and others ignore errors entirely.
- **Whether spaces count.** Most tests include spaces in the character count, but not all do.
- **Test length.** Short tests tend to give higher numbers than long ones, because it is easier to stay focused for 30 seconds than for five minutes.
- **The text itself.** Common lowercase words are easier than sentences with capitals, punctuation and numbers.
- **Rounding and timing.** Small differences in when the timer starts and how numbers are rounded can shift results by a word or two.

The most reliable way to track your progress is to use the same test every time and compare your results with each other, not with scores from a different site.

## How QwertyRank calculates your score

Here is exactly how the [QwertyRank typing test](/en) measures you:

- **WPM** counts only correct characters, divided by five and scaled to one minute. In practice, that means the characters of each word you typed correctly, plus the space after it. A word left with a mistake in it does not add to your WPM.
- **Raw WPM** counts all the characters you typed, correct or not, except those you deleted, using the same five-character word.
- **Accuracy** is correct keystrokes divided by total keystrokes, including the mistakes you later corrected.

The ranked test lasts 30 seconds, after a 3-second countdown, so your WPM is the correct characters from those 30 seconds scaled up to a full minute. For example, if you type 200 correct characters in 30 seconds, that is 40 words in half a minute, or 80 WPM.

QwertyRank shows WPM rather than CPM, but you can always get your CPM by multiplying your WPM by five. In the example above, 80 WPM is 400 CPM.

To enter a leaderboard you need at least 90% accuracy, so a fast but sloppy game will not count. There is one leaderboard per language and keyboard type, and only your best game appears.

## Which number should you focus on?

For everyday purposes, net WPM is the number that matters most, because it reflects how much correct text you can produce. Raw WPM is useful as a diagnostic: it shows your potential if you cleaned up your mistakes. Accuracy tells you whether you are pushing too hard.

A healthy pattern is a net WPM close to your raw WPM and accuracy in the mid-90s or higher. If you want to know how your number compares with typical ranges, see our guide on [average typing speed](/en/guides/average-typing-speed).
